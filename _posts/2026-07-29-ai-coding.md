It is (unfortunately) undeniable that artificial intelligence is a unique and powerful tool in computer science and software engineering. As someone who both simultaneously uses AI sometimes in his work and occasionally in his own projects, and someone who has been quite verbose about the issues and the exploits that come with artificial intelligence, I hate myself. But, should I?

*This is quite a long post, so if you want to see my opinion on AI coding, scroll to the bottom.*

## The Future of Software Engineering

There has been a lot of talk about whether the future of Software Engineering is doomed, and whether software engineers will be replaced by artificial intelligence in the near future. It is my firm belief that this is impossible, both in the sense of accountability, and in the sense of human-centred computing.

It is not possible to let artificial intelligence make every single decision, at least not without human supervision and oversight. The cliché here is obviously the famous quote from the 1979 IBM training manual, that:

> A computer can never be held accountable; Therefore, a computer must never make a management decision.

Although this quote is from some 47 years ago, it still holds value to this day. We cannot trust a computer to make decisions, especially not such a volatile tool like artificial intelligence, which in its current state outputs random nonsense from time to time, and its effectiveness may vary by model and by prompt.

When I was at Monash University's launch of the new Bachelor of CyberAI, I was obviously excited by the opportunity to be there in the first place, but also a sense of doom was imposed upon me by the emphasis on artificial intelligence; if the future is AI, then what are we doing here? Spending upwards of $50,000 on a degree in computer science?

Then, I realised that the existence of a degree such as the Bachelor of CyberAI implies that humans will not be replaced by artificial intelligence in software engineering, in the sense that AI will open up new avenues for software engineers and computer scientists, and that AI is but a tool, and to compare yourself to a tool is quite dehumanising.

A lot of this will sound like cope, but the truth is there will always be a human behind every AI model, every AI company and every AI nonsense someone may launch. If one day an AI were to launch its own company, and make its own models, and somehow is completely managed by AI, and if we ignore the obvious fact that someone would have had to prompt or lead the AI to do something like that, we should recognise that AI itself does not create value, AI agents do not create value, it is the humans who use the AI to do something, or to purchase something that actually provides and creates value. Hence the role of AI in human-centred computing, and a technology created by humans can ultimately only be useful, effective and used by a human.

## The Bubble

Now, I am not an economist, but even I can see something sketchy going on here. I do not want to discuss the economy, the cost of living, politics or stocks; I don't have a qualification in these topics, and I want to refrain from talking about them.

But it seems that the circulation of money between the large AI companies (e.g. NVIDIA, Anthropic, OpenAI) is quite suspicious [1]. I am not alone when I challenge the sustainability of this so-called "AI economy," with $30bn ~ $40bn invested into artificial intelligence, of which 95% see no return on investment [2]; I fear that a future in which we all prosper under this brand new technology will never come.

I have never respected any stockbrokers, or anyone who makes a living out of the stock market, simply out of my disdain for careers that create no value for society. This arbitrary, unmanaged and uncontrolled desire for tangible wealth, even despite a comfortable life, will never earn my respect. I understand the desire to "move up in society," but at what point does this cross an enormous ethical line? When we pour an unfathomable amount of money and resources which we could have spent on other things into a technology that may never return a single dollar to the needy, what are we doing as a society?

Don't get me wrong, Generative AI is an extremely powerful technology, but this rapid and unfettered commercialisation will eventually cause a serious issue for society. Perhaps, the question that we ask should not be "how much money can I make if I do X," but rather "how much impact can I create if I do X." Jobs, careers, technologies should be about producing value, and AI can be used for good. Certainly, AI cannot do everything for us, as we discussed earlier; a human-centred technology has to be manned, and it is up to the programmers of the world to perhaps attempt to create impact with AI, instead of money.

## The Polarisation

There are often two kinds of opinions surrounding artificial intelligence. Some people completely despise AI and AI-generated code, which I have done for quite a bit; and those who praise and glaze every single thing AI has ever done, and unfortunately the latter is quite a common occurrence.

Every time I log on to LinkedIn or talk to particular people about AI, I die a little bit. A friend of mine, with the help of AI, created 8 Python files and wondered if programmers could do this without AI, which has been the greatest insult to the career choice I have made to date.

We are all so easily impressed by things we do not inherently understand; it is part of human nature. We all like to feel good about ourselves, and we all like to think that we are the most efficient "machines," though we are often not.

But, at every turn, some people are so proud of using AI and seem to think of actual programmers who have often dedicated decades of their lives to mastering their craft as lesser beings, and that they are somehow doing things incorrectly or inefficiently. There is something that comes with learning something and trying to master something, and that is the ridding of your own ego, of being humble and understanding that there are people better than you, and you must accept this fact and learn from them.

The mastering of a craft, whether art or not, in the end, no matter what the presentation, the execution or any of the results may look like, so long as you put your time and effort into it and you are growing from it, is pure, and it is egoless. It is a sacred fruit which has been blessed upon humanity for generations and generations, yet we are tossing it all away for what? LinkedIn likes? It is incredibly disrespectful, whether in a conversation about AI or not, to discuss other people's work as if yours are better, simply based on human decency and the fact that you do not grow from the fall of others.

Perhaps, I am generalising; And, perhaps my sights are too narrow. But there exists a balance between glazing AI and demonising AI. Perhaps, in an age of AI, the best way to deal with AI is to accept AI.

## Closing Thoughts

I think we should accept AI as a tool, not as a replacement for genuine human effort, but as a supplement. We live in a rapidly changing world, and whether we like it or not, the world will move with or without us.

The discussion of whether AI will change software engineering is futile on both ends. For the haters, AI has already changed software engineering from the ground up, from how we learn, from how we code and how we debug. For the lovers, AI is a volatile and unsustainable technology, from both an economic and environmental standpoint, and the "distant future" where AI can solve all of humanity's problems (AGI) will likely never come.

Humanity is not doomed; software engineering is not doomed. Things may get worse, but unfortunately there is not much we can do except to raise the question, "what impact and what value can I create in the age of AI?"

**There is nothing wrong with using AI to generate code, but everything is wrong with using AI and disrespecting the craft of programming, which many spend decades to master.**
